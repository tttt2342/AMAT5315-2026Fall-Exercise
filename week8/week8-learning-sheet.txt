Week 8: Language models and the transformer
Jin-Guo Liu, Xi-Wei Pan
Abstract: We learn how a language model assigns probabilities to text and generates new text. We look inside a 
pretrained table that turns tokens into vectors and find relations between words stored as directions. We predict each 
token of short children’s stories from the ones before it, first by counting, which the curse of dimensionality limits to 
short contexts, then with an MLP. We derive the MLP as a memory of key–value facts written into its weights, and 
measure how many facts such a memory holds. Facts stated in the text itself cannot be written into fixed weights, so 
we derive attention as a memory built from the context. We assemble the two blocks into a transformer, train it on the 
stories and sample new ones. Finally, we refit the Chinchilla scaling law to published training runs and find how to split 
a compute budget between model size and data. The Goal lines state the numerical targets.

Tokens and the tokenizer. A token is the basic unit a model reads and writes: an integer standing for a piece of text. 
The vocabulary is the set of all 𝑉 tokens. A tokenizer is trained once on a fixed corpus, a collection of texts, which fixes 
its vocabulary. It then encodes any string into tokens and decodes tokens back into the string. A byte-pair encoding starts 
from single characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token until the vocabulary 
is full, so frequent pieces such as “ the” or “ time” become single tokens. We download one that Karpathy trained on 
TinyStories, with 𝑉 = 512 tokens. Three are special, among them the end-of-sequence token that separates stories. 
Another 256 stand for single bytes, a fallback that lets any text be encoded. The remaining 253 are merged pieces of text.
The normalizer of a language model. Week 6′s energy models needed a normalizer 𝑍, a sum over all configurations 
that we could not compute. A language model avoids it: each prediction is a probability distribution over a finite set, 
normalized by a sum we can compute. Parts 2 to 5 rely on this.
The path through the week. Figure 2 shows the six Parts as one chain. Part 1 turns tokens into vectors a model can 
read. Parts 2 and 3 predict the next token, first by counting and then with an MLP, which stores facts in its weights. A 
fact stated only in the current text never reaches those weights. Part 4 builds a second memory from the context itself, 
attention. Part 5 combines the two blocks into a transformer, trains it on the stories, and samples from it. Part 6 asks 
how the loss improves with model size and data, and how to spend a compute budget.

The pretrained table.  potion-base-8M  from Model2Vec [1] is one such matrix, with 𝑉 = 29,528 rows and 𝑑 =
256 columns, a 30 MB file. Its tokens are word pieces, and common words such as “cat” and “january” are a single token 
each, so their vector is a single row. The table was distilled from a sentence-embedding network trained on far more 
text than TinyStories: words used in similar sentences were pushed to similar rows. The nearest rows to “cat”, by the 
angle between vectors, are “cats”, “kitty” and “kitten”.
Relations as directions. A pair of words such as (france, paris) has an offset, the vector 𝐸[𝑏] − 𝐸[𝑎] from the first 
word to the second, with each row first scaled to length one. If the table stores the relation “country to capital” as one 
direction, the offsets of different pairs point the same way. We test each pair against the others: the cosine between its 
offset and the average offset of the other eight pairs. It is the cosine of the angle between the two vectors: 1 when the 
offset points along the others, at 0°, and near 0 when it is unrelated to them, at 90°. Leaving the pair out of the average 
keeps it from agreeing with itself. Two random directions in 256 dimensions have a cosine of about ± 1
16, so a mean 
near 0.5 is far from chance. Mikolov and coauthors [2] found such parallel offsets in word vectors trained on news text.
The shuffled control. Shuffling the rows keeps every vector and every distance between vectors, but gives each word 
another word’s vector. Any pattern that survives the shuffle comes from the vectors alone, not from what the words 
mean. A pattern counts as meaning only when the control loses it.
KEY CONCEPTS
• Embedding table: one vector per token; a lookup is a one-hot vector times the table.
• Offset and cosine: a relation is a direction when each pair’s offset points along the average offset of the other 
pairs.
• Shuffled control: the same vectors on the wrong words, so meaning is lost.

KEY CONCEPTS
• Chain rule: a sequence’s probability is a product of next-token probabilities, so the model is normalized.
• Markov chain and n-gram: the n-gram model keeps the last 𝑛− 1 tokens; the bigram is the simple Markov

• Likelihood and smoothing: row-normalized counts maximize the training likelihood; add-k smoothing keeps 
every probability positive.
• Cross-entropy, KL divergence, perplexity:  the held-out negative log-likelihood per token is the text’s 
entropy plus the model’s KL divergence; its exponential counts the equally likely next tokens.
• Ancestral sampling: draw each token from the model given those already drawn.
• Curse of dimensionality: 𝑉 𝑛−1 contexts outgrow any training set, so long-context counts are mostly empty.

The one-token MLP and the bigram.  The model sees exactly what the bigram sees. It must write the bigram table, 
one next-token distribution for each token, into its weights. No model that sees one token can predict better than the 
bigram, so a well-trained MLP reaches the bigram’s held-out loss, neither better nor worse. This is a check on training, 
not a feature of the network.
Training with AdamW. An optimizer turns gradients into weight updates. Week 7′s Adam scaled each update by 
running averages of the gradient and its square. AdamW adds weight decay, a small pull of every weight toward zero. 
Each step updates the weights from one batch of examples. Large early updates can throw a freshly started network off 
course, so warmup raises the learning rate from near zero over the first steps. Cosine decay then lowers it along half a 
cosine period, so the last updates are small. The learning rate in a prompt is the peak between the two.
Facts stated only in the context. After training, the weights stay fixed. A fact that appears only in the current story, 
such as the dog’s name, was never written into them. A memory for such facts must be written by the text itself. Part 
4 builds it.

Residuals and layer norm. A residual connection adds a sublayer’s output to its input, 𝑥 ← 𝑥+ 𝑓(𝑥), so each block 
refines the current vectors and gradients pass through deep stacks. Layer norm rescales each vector to mean zero and 
variance one across its entries, then applies a learned scale and shift 𝑔,𝑏. Applying it before each sublayer, the pre-norm 
form, keeps training stable:
𝑥 ← 𝑥+ Attention(LN1(𝑥)), 𝑥 ← 𝑥+ MLP(LN2(𝑥)). (21)
Multi-head attention.  Multi-head attention splits the width 𝑑 into ℎ heads of width 𝑑
ℎ, runs attention in each 
separately, and concatenates the results. Different heads can look for different things. Each head divides its scores by 
the square root of its own width.
The causal mask.  A next-token model must not see the future. Causal attention sets 𝑎𝑡𝑗 = 0 for every 𝑗 > 𝑡, by 
excluding those positions from the sum (Figure 11, right).
The MLP sublayer. The MLP widens each vector to 4𝑑 entries, applies GELU, a smooth version of max(0,𝑢), and 
maps back to 𝑑 entries. It is the memory store of Part 3, with 4𝑑 memories.
Position vectors. Part 4 showed that attention ignores order. Text needs order: “dog bites man” differs from “man bites 
dog”. Adding a learned position vector 𝑃[𝑡] to the token embedding at position 𝑡 makes the same token at different 
positions look different.
Training on every position.  A training example is a run of 65 consecutive tokens. The model reads the first 64 
and predicts, at every position, the next token. Because attention is causal, each position’s prediction uses only earlier 
tokens, so one forward pass trains 64 predictions. The loss is their average cross-entropy.
Loss at each position. The average over the 64 positions hides how much context helps. Position 𝑡 of a window has 
read 𝑡 tokens, so the mean loss at position 𝑡 over many windows shows what the model gains from each further token 
it reads. Kaplan and coauthors [12, Fig. 20] plotted this curve for large models and found that it keeps falling across the 
whole context. A bigram reads 1 token and a trigram 2, so their loss is the same at every position.
The learning-rate schedule. As in Part 3, warmup raises the learning rate from zero, here linearly over the first 300 
steps, and cosine decay then lowers it, here to a tenth of its peak by the last step.
Generating text. Generation is ancestral sampling, as in Part 2: compute the next-token distribution from the tokens 
so far, draw one token, append it, repeat. The model returns logits 𝑧𝑣. A sampler decides how to draw from them.

Surprise and entropy of the model’s own samples.  The surprise of a token is − ln 𝑝(𝑣). Its average under the

is less surprising than the model predicts. Dividing the difference by its standard error over independent sequences 
gives a z-score, whose size beyond about 4 signals a real mismatch.
KEY CONCEPTS
• Block: pre-norm attention and MLP sublayers, each wrapped in a residual.
• Multi-head attention: several narrower attentions side by side.
• Causal mask: weights on later positions are exactly zero.
• Position vectors: they make the same token at different positions look different.
• Training: causal attention lets one pass train a prediction at every position.
• Schedule: warmup, then cosine decay of the learning rate.
• Temperature: divides logits by 𝑇 ; 𝑇 → 0 is greedy.
• Top-k, top-p, min-p: drop unlikely tokens, then renormalize.
• Self-consistency: at 𝑇 = 1, mean surprise equals mean entropy.
