Week 7: Diffusion and flow matching
Jin-Guo Liu, Xi-Wei Pan
Abstract: We learn to generate data from Gaussian noise, starting with four groups of points, called clusters, whose 
distribution we can check exactly. We train a noise predictor, compare sampling methods, then use a downloaded 
predictor to generate photographs and fill missing pixels.
Diffusion models
Generating data from Gaussian noise.  Week 6 generated samples from an energy model without computing its 
normalizer 𝑍. This week learns to transform fresh random numbers into data. A generative model learns a rule for 
drawing new examples; drawing an example is sampling, and the result is a sample. We use a neural network, a function 
with adjustable parameters. Training fits those parameters to examples.
Gaussian noise as the starting distribution. We start generation with independent draws from the standard Gaussian 
𝒩︀(0,1), centred at zero with variance one: its average squared distance from zero is one. We draw coordinates 
independently, so knowing one draw does not change another’s probabilities. Added random disturbances are noise a 
clean sample 𝑥0 is a data sample before this noise is added. Its coordinates may store a point’s position or an image’s 
pixel colours.
Forward and reverse diffusion. The fixed process called forward diffusion repeatedly shrinks 𝑥0 toward zero and 
adds Gaussian noise. Write the current state as 𝑥𝑡 after 𝑡 steps. The step index is the noise level, also called the diffusion 
level here the final level is 𝑇 = 1000. In Figure 1, read the top row left to right to see data become noise. Generation 
follows the reverse process, reading the lower rows right to left.
Figure 1: The forward and reverse processes. Top: forward diffusion turns four clusters into an approximately Gaussian cloud. Middle: a

Generation by small reverse steps. Starting from fresh Gaussian noise at 𝑡= 𝑇, a learned rule repeatedly moves to 
a lower level until it reaches 𝑡= 0. The positions form a path or trajectory; the first and last are its endpoints. The aim 
1

is to recover the data distribution: which outcomes are common and which are rare. It does not recover the particular 
sample that began a forward run. Estimating a clean sample from a noisy one is denoising.
Noise prediction and DDPM sampling. Add noise to a clean sample and train a noise predictor to estimate the added 
noise. The known answer is the training target; fitting numerical targets is regression. A denoising diffusion probabilistic 
model (DDPM) uses the prediction to choose a reverse step and adds fresh randomness. This generation rule is the 
sampler.
Two routes to fewer sampling steps. Each reverse step evaluates a network. Part 2 reuses the noise predictor with 
a denoising diffusion implicit model (DDIM). Its deterministic update makes no random choice after the starting draw. 
DDPM’s random steps are called stochastic. Part 3 trains a different network by flow matching: it predicts a point’s 
velocity, how fast and in which direction the point moves. We compare the number of steps these samplers need to 
recover the four clusters.

Conditional-mean prediction and the remaining training loss. The noisy point 𝑥𝑡 = (0,0) could have come from 
any cluster. The predictor cannot identify which noise was added. A conditional density 𝑝(𝑢 | 𝑦) describes an unknown 
𝑢 after observing 𝑦; the bar means “given”. Its probability-weighted average is the conditional mean, 𝔼[𝑢 | 𝑦]. For noise 
prediction, 𝑢 = 𝜀 and 𝑦 = 𝑥𝑡, at a fixed 𝑡. At the origin, symmetry makes the mean noise zero.
Let 𝑎 be any prediction made from 𝑦. Its average squared error separates into two terms:
𝔼[‖𝑢− 𝑎‖2 | 𝑦] = 𝔼[‖𝑢− 𝔼[𝑢 | 𝑦]‖2 | 𝑦] + ‖𝔼[𝑢 | 𝑦]− 𝑎‖2. (10)
The first is the uncertainty left after observing 𝑦. The second measures how far the prediction is from the conditional 
mean. Only the second depends on 𝑎, so choosing 𝑎 = 𝔼[𝑢 | 𝑦] minimizes the error. Noise-prediction training therefore 
aims at 𝔼[𝜀 | 𝑥𝑡] at each level. The first term can remain positive even for the best predictor. It explains why the training 
loss need not reach zero.
With this exact prediction, the sampler’s mean would be exact; a learned predictor approximates it. Treating the reverse 
distribution as Gaussian around the mean is a separate approximation, justified by small steps.
Why the mixture sampler must not clip to [−1, 1]. Replacing values below a lower bound by that bound, and 
values above an upper bound by that bound, is clipping. Clipping to [−1,1] would exclude this mixture’s four centres, 
so we omit it.
KEY CONCEPTS
• Target: equal cluster populations, prescribed centres and coordinate spread 0.3.
• Forward diffusion: shrink and add Gaussian noise; the schedule sets its variance.
• Learning: squared noise error trains a time-conditioned predictor toward the conditional mean.

Flow matching targets the average path velocity.  Different pairs can pass through the same position at the same

Why the average velocity transports the target distribution.  A velocity field assigns a velocity 𝑣(𝑥,𝑠) to each

Convolutions extract local image features. The image predictor uses convolutions: each slides a learned filter over
