Week 3: Monte Carlo simulation
Jin-Guo Liu, Xi-Wei Pan
Abstract: The two-dimensional Ising model is exactly solved, so any sampler that claims to simulate it can be held to 
Onsager’s answer. We model the spin system as a Markov chain and derive from detailed balance both the single-flip 
Metropolis rule and the Wolff cluster rule that flips whole domains at once. From the chain’s samples we measure the 
magnetization curve, locate the critical temperature on finite lattices with an error bar that accounts for the chain’s 
memory, and find that cluster flips remove the critical slowing down that defeats single flips at the transition. The 
evidence is a tested Rust tool, a run anyone can repeat, and a recording of the lattice as it heats. Together they show 
that a Monte Carlo estimate is only as good as its sampler, its finite-size extrapolation, and its error analysis.

The Ising model
The Ising model is the course’s reference problem, and every Part of this week samples it.  It is the simplest 
system that has a phase transition: a magnet that holds its magnetization when cold and loses it at a sharp temperature 
when heated, as iron does at its Curie point. The same lattice, read differently, is a binary alloy ordering or a gas 
condensing on a lattice. It is the course’s reference problem because it is exactly solved: there is a formula to check every 
number against, and a check that fails when the physics is wrong is what this course grades.

Domains and the correlation length. A domain is a connected region of spins that agree. Cold, one domain fills the 
lattice and only islands dissent. Hot, domains are small and short-lived. At 𝑇𝑐 domains of every size coexist, from single 
sites to the whole lattice, because the correlation length, the distance over which spins still know about each other, has 
grown to the size of the lattice. Part 1 records this.

So the key is to design a good sampler: a procedure that draws configurations with probability 𝑃 (𝑠) itself. A Markov 
chain is such a sampler: a random walk whose next state depends only on the current one. Write 𝑊 (𝑠 → 𝑠′) for the 
probability of stepping from 𝑠 to 𝑠′. This week builds two chains for the same 𝑃 : single spin flips now, and cluster flips 
in Part 4.
Detailed balance and ergodicity. A distribution is stationary when one step of the walk leaves it unchanged: a crowd 
of walkers spread as 𝑃  is still spread as 𝑃  after everyone moves. Detailed balance is the sufficient condition that makes 
𝑃  stationary,
𝑃 (𝑠)𝑊 (𝑠 → 𝑠′) = 𝑃 (𝑠′)𝑊 (𝑠′ → 𝑠), (6)
the traffic from 𝑠 to 𝑠′ equals the traffic back, so no configuration gains or loses walkers. Ergodicity is the second 
condition: every configuration must be reachable from every other in a finite number of steps, so the chain cannot get 
stuck in a corner of the space ( Figure 3) and the stationary distribution is unique. Convergence from any start also 
requires aperiodicity: the walk must not be locked into a repeating cycle of fixed length. Rejected Metropolis proposals 
provide self-transitions (a step that keeps the current configuration) at the finite positive temperatures used here. These 
conditions make the chain converge to 𝑃 , and its states become the samples that Equation 5 needs. Two consequences 
run through the week: a run must first equilibrate, discarding the steps before the chain has converged, and how fast it 
converges is a property of the sampler, measured in Part 3 and improved in Part 4.

The ramp recording. The usage line of the contract runs one ramp through the whole transition and records frames 
of the lattice, because what changes at the transition is the size of the domains, not the number of up spins; the lattice 
shows it directly. Each temperature starts from the final lattice of the previous one, a warm start, which saves reaching

Near 𝑇𝑐 single flips need of order 𝐿𝑧 sweeps per independent configuration, so running longer loses the race against a

The Wolff cluster update [2] flips many spins at once. Pick a site at random. Grow a cluster outward from it: for each 
neighbour that has the same spin as the site it was reached from, add it to the cluster with probability
𝑝 = 1 − 𝑒−2/𝑇 , (16)
and never add a neighbour of opposite spin. Repeat for the neighbours of every newly added site until no site is added. 
Then flip the whole cluster. There is no acceptance test: the flip always happens. Bonds inside the cluster and outside 
it keep their energy; only the declined bonds at its boundary change, and Equation 16 is exactly the probability that 
makes acceptance 1 satisfy Equation 6.
The observation interval.  One cluster move grows and flips one cluster. Record after every move. A cluster’s size 
depends on the configuration; stopping only when accumulated sizes reach 𝐿2 changes the observed distribution. The 
observation interval must not depend on the sizes just seen. Keeping every move also preserves the correlations needed 
for error estimates.

Challenge: beat Wolff at the critical point
Neural samplers. The update you built in Part 4 is the state of the art of 1989. Since 2019 a line of work has replaced 
the chain’s proposal with a neural network that draws a whole lattice at once, instead of one spin or one cluster. Four 
families are in use. An autoregressive network writes the spins one after another, each conditioned on the ones already 
written. A normalizing flow maps easy random numbers through an invertible network into a lattice. A transformer is 
a network whose layers let every site attend to every other. A diffusion model starts from noise and removes it in steps. 
None of these is exact on its own, so the samples are corrected in one of two ways. The first keeps a Markov chain 
and applies the acceptance test of Equation 7 to the proposed lattice, which gives a Metropolis-corrected neural chain. 
The second keeps every sample and weights it by the ratio of the Boltzmann probability to the probability the network 
assigned it, which is neural importance sampling; its figure of merit is the effective sample size, the fraction of drawn 
samples that count as independent. The promise of both is a proposal whose cost does not grow at 𝑇𝑐, and the free 
energy as a by-product, which no Monte Carlo chain gives you.
The published record on the two-dimensional Ising model at 𝑇𝑐, checked on 2026-09-06, is short. The largest 
Metropolis-corrected neural chain is 𝐿 = 128, and it reports only a relative autocorrelation statement [6]. The largest 
importance-sampled run is 𝐿 = 180, with an effective sample size of 0.59 after 192 hours of training, no Metropolis 
step, and the authors’ own verdict that neural samplers are not competitive with cluster algorithms [7]. No paper has 
fitted a dynamic exponent for a neural chain; an absolute 𝜏int exists only at 𝐿 = 16 [8]. No paper has counted training 
plus sampling cost against Wolff in two dimensions at any size, and the one such count, in three dimensions at 𝐿 =
8, came out about 70 × in Wolff’s favour [9]. Critical slowing down does not disappear in these methods. It moves 
into training, where the learning time of a one-layer score network grows as 𝐿2 at criticality [10], and the importance 
weights can look healthy while whole modes of the distribution are missing [11].
