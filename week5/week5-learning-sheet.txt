Week 5: Automatic differentiation and checkpointing
Jin-Guo Liu, Xi-Wei Pan
Abstract: Each Part defines the terms this abstract uses. This week we learn to differentiate a time-stepping simulation 
with automatic differentiation and to run its reverse pass within a storage budget. We first write the forward and reverse 
passes of a pair energy by hand in JAX, compare them with the analytic derivative and with finite differences, and read 
the graphs JAX records. We then build an acoustic wave solver and differentiate it through time with Enzyme: forward 
mode gives the Born scattered data of a thin reflector, reverse mode back-propagates those data into an image that 
locates the reflector, and the transpose identity checks one against the other. Treeverse checkpointing then replaces 
the 241 saved states of the full trajectory by six while leaving the image unchanged, and the same operators image the 
dipping layers of a Marmousi model.
Resources:
• Liu and Xu, Automatic differentiation and its applications in physics simulation [1]. Parts 1, 3 and 4 identify the relevant 
reading.
• Symes, Reverse time migration with optimal checkpointing [2]. Parts 3 and 4 develop its modeling, adjoint calculation 
and imaging experiment.
The problem: imaging underground reflectors
Survey vocabulary. A source injects a pressure pulse into the ground. The resulting pressure field describes the wave 
amplitude at each position and time. A receiver samples pressure at a fixed position. One source activation is a shot. 
The pressure one receiver records over time is its trace. The traces of one shot form a shot gather. The background is 
the reference wave-speed field. A velocity perturbation is a change in that speed. It scatters the incident wave toward 
the receivers.
Reverse time migration.  Reverse time migration , or RTM, turns recorded echoes into a picture of the reflectors 
underground. A forward run sends the source pulse into the background; its pressure field over time is the wavefield. 
A reverse run replays the scattered traces backward in time from the receivers. At each point, RTM combines the two 
fields into how much the data would change with the wave speed there, summed over time: the image. It is large where 
the two waves meet, at a reflector.

Finite differences. A finite difference estimates the derivative from nearby energies, with a small step ℎ:
𝐷ℎ𝑈(𝑟)= 𝑈(𝑟 + ℎ)− 𝑈(𝑟 − ℎ)
2ℎ . (7)
Its truncation error is proportional to ℎ2 for a smooth function. Its cancellation error grows as ℎ shrinks: the two energies 
agree in more leading digits, so their difference keeps fewer of the 16 significant digits that a 64-bit number stores. This 
rounding error is near 10−16/ℎ. The two errors balance where ℎ3 ≈ 10−16, so the best step lies between 10−6 and 10−5.
The chain rule in a program. Automatic differentiation, or AD, applies the chain rule to the operations the program 
executes. The original calculation is the primal. AD differentiates that discrete calculation. Its remaining error comes 
from floating-point arithmetic alone.

The graph an AD library records. JAX is a Python library for automatic differentiation; the agent writes the JAX code. 
Like other AD libraries, it runs a function once and records each operation it executes: the computational graph. JAX 
calls this record a jaxpr, one line per operation, and prints it with jax.make_jaxpr . Its gradient function, jax.grad , 
differentiates that record in reverse mode. JAX computes with 32-bit floats, which keep about seven digits, unless 64-
bit floats are enabled. The checks below need at least twelve.

KEY CONCEPTS
• A force is the negative derivative of pair energy.
• Finite differences balance truncation error against cancellation.
• Forward mode propagates tangents with JVPs; reverse mode propagates adjoints with VJPs and adds contribu-
tions to shared values.
• The whole Jacobian costs one forward pass per input or one reverse pass per output; a scalar gradient needs one 
reverse pass and its retained primal values.
• The gradient of a cluster energy costs a few energy evaluations in reverse mode and grows in proportion to its 
3𝑁 inputs in forward mode.
• JAX records the computational graph; 64-bit floats keep about sixteen digits.
• The check: compare both hand-written modes and a finite difference with the analytic derivative, find the

The storage problem.  Checkpointing saves selected states and recreates the others by recomputation, running the 
forward update again. A checkpoint budget limits the saved states. Our convention reserves the initial state separately: 
𝛿 extra slots permit at most 𝛿 + 1 saved complete states. A save copies a complete state into a slot. A restore copies it 
back into the working state. Replay advances that state to the time the reverse step needs. Replay adds nothing to the 
image; only the reverse calls accumulate the velocity sensitivity. A saved state also carries its time index, so replay uses 
the correct source pulse.

The Marmousi model. Marmousi is a synthetic geological velocity model with curved and dipping layers, a faulted 
zone and a fast basement. Following Symes, we smooth it to obtain the background 𝑐0 and use its shorter-scale structure 
as the reflectivity 𝑚. Our copy is the licensed model distributed with ADSeismic, a Julia package for differentiable

KEY CONCEPTS
• Checkpointing keeps a few complete states within a checkpoint budget; save, restore and replay supply the 
others.
• Treeverse’s recursive construction assigns the budget.
• A smaller budget costs more forward steps and lowers the peak saved states, while the relative L2 error of the 
image stays zero.
• An action file lists every store, restore, call, grad and fetch.
• On Marmousi, six states replace 4.16 GB of full history; the raw image loses strength with depth.
• The check: compare the checkpointed image with full history, read the action plot and the work curve, then 
find the dipping layers in the Marmousi image.
